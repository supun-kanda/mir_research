Beat-tracking under rubato

Beat tracking means recovering the times of the beats — the pulse you'd tap your foot to. Almost every beat tracker carries an implicit assumption of roughly steady tempo, and that assumption is exactly what expressive classical playing violates. Rubato is deliberate tempo fluctuation for expression: pushing forward, holding back, ritardando at cadences. So the question is really "how much does this hidden steady-tempo assumption cost us when it's false, and does it cost the two trackers differently?"

The two contestants embody two eras. librosa's beat_track builds an onset-strength envelope and runs Ellis-style dynamic programming that trades onset evidence against a global tempo prior — it actively penalizes departures from one estimated tempo, so it's structurally hostile to rubato. madmom uses an RNN to produce a beat-activation function, then a dynamic Bayesian network / HMM to decode beat times, which permits tempo to drift within learned bounds. The expected result is that both degrade as rubato increases but librosa falls off a cliff while madmom bends.

ASAP is the right dataset because it's built from score-aligned performances, so you get ground-truth beat times even under heavy rubato — the beats come from the score alignment, not from assuming steadiness. Audio exists for the MAESTRO subset; use that for the audio trackers. You score with mir_eval.beat.f_measure (matches estimated to reference beats within ±70 ms). The "slice by tempo deviation" step is what turns this from a leaderboard into a finding: compute a per-piece rubato measure — e.g. the standard deviation of inter-beat intervals, or local tempo variance — then plot F-measure against it. The shape of that curve is the paper-in-miniature.
